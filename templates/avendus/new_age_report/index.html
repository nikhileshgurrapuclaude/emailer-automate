Dear Reader,

The global economy is entering a new phase of industrial investment. After two decades in which capital and talent flowed towards software, they are returning to hard, physical technology. Artificial intelligence, the energy transition and a sharper focus on national security are all driving demand for semiconductors, batteries, satellites, drones, robots and defence systems. At the same time, governments and companies are redrawing supply chains around resilience as much as cost.

In our latest report, From Factory to Frontier: Building India's New-Age Industrial Stack, we explore what this shift means for India. Our conclusion is that India has both a pressing reason to build and a credible opportunity to compete.

India imports nearly all of its advanced logic chips and lithium-ion cells, and 85-90% of its rare-earth magnets. Yet 2026 has delivered the first real proof points:

a. India's first private orbital launch.

b. Its first chip assembly-and-test plants in commercial production.

c. Indian warehouse-robotics platforms deploying overseas.

We define New-Age Industrials as the emerging part of India's hard-tech economy, where competitive advantage is built on proprietary technology, deep process know-how or tightly integrated hardware and software. The opportunity is substantial but selective. It is strongest where sovereign demand, domestic scale, engineering depth and cost competitiveness come together.

Against this backdrop, the report explores the key themes shaping the sector:

1. From bits to atoms: Deep tech's share of global venture funding has tripled in a decade, from 11% to 33%. State programmes such as the US CHIPS Act (USD 52.7 bn) are funding hard tech at scale.

2. Manufacturing is the moat: Deep Unit costs of launch, batteries and solar have fallen by 98–99.9%. The binding constraint has moved to making critical inputs reliably and at scale. That know-how compounds and is hard to replicate.

3. Security sets the demand floor: Global military spending reached a record USD 2.9 tn in 2025. In India, 75% of defence capital acquisition is reserved for domestic industry.

4. Policy has moved upstream: India's incentives are shifting from final assembly to components and materials and increasingly pay only for what is actually produced. Examples include cell incentives, solar approved-list mandates and a INR 72.8 bn rare-earth magnet scheme.

5. The next three to five years will decide the winners: Companies that own their technology, have a sovereign or large anchor customer, and convert qualification into repeat orders and exports are best placed to build durable positions.

The report takes a deep dive into six sectors: Defence and Aerospace, Space Tech, Robotics and Automation, Semiconductors, Energy Transition and Advanced Materials. For each, it unpacks the value chain, the shifts reshaping it, India's current capabilities and the sub-segments where India has a right to win.
